How to actually tell

None of these methods are perfect on their own. Scammers get better every month — so layer a few of these together, especially before trusting a photo tied to money, identity verification, or a relationship.

Tools you can use right now

  • Check an image (on this site) — reads the AI labels that ChatGPT, Gemini, Adobe Firefly and other tools write into their images (Content Credentials and metadata), plus any camera details. It runs in your browser, so your image is never uploaded. An AI label is strong evidence. No label proves nothing: screenshots, re-saving, and most social media and messaging apps strip them, and camera details can be copied or faked.
  • SynthID Detector (Google) — checks for invisible watermarks that survive screenshots and re-saving. Detects images from Google, OpenAI, NVIDIA, and Kakao tools. Free with a Google account; daily limits apply.
  • ZeroGPT AI Image Detector — gives a likelihood score based on how the image looks. Treat the score as a hint, not a verdict.
  • Reverse image search — Google Images or TinEye. Finds where else the photo appears online. If the "person" turns out to be a stock photo, uses a different name, or shows up on a dozen dating profiles, that's your answer.

SynthID, ZeroGPT, and reverse image search upload your image to their servers. Don't send them photos you wouldn't want to share.

What to look for with your own eyes

These tells catch older and cheaper fakes, but newer AI tools often get them right. Spotting one is a good reason for suspicion; a photo that looks clean is not evidence it's real.

  • Hands, ears, and teeth — extra/missing fingers, asymmetric earrings, teeth that blur together.
  • Text in the background (signs, labels, books) that turns into gibberish.
  • Backgrounds that smear, repeat, or don't quite make physical sense (mismatched reflections, impossible shadows).
  • Skin that looks uniformly smooth/waxy, or hair that blends into the background without flyaways.
  • Glasses or jewelry that are subtly asymmetric between left and right.

For romance scams & "KYC" verification specifically

  • A live video call, or a photo doing a specific gesture right now, can expose a cheap fake, but it no longer proves anything. Scammers now use real-time face-swapping on live calls, and attackers feed deepfake video straight into identity checks. See real cases.
  • Be suspicious of a profile where every photo looks studio-perfect or inconsistent in lighting/background from shot to shot.
  • Never send money, crypto, or gift cards to someone you've only met online, however real they look on camera.
  • Never treat a photo (or even a video call) as sufficient identity verification on its own when money, credentials, or access are involved — pair it with an independent channel (known phone number, official company directory, in-person check).
